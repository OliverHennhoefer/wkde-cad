Conformal anomaly detection integrates anomaly scoring, the calibration of resulting scores into statistical measures of evidence, and downstream error control within a unified inference pipeline. Although it rests on the same distribution-free finite-sample validity under exchangeability as conformal prediction, conformal anomaly detection is inferentially more demanding: calibration is not the endpoint of inference, but an intermediate evidence layer that must support statistically controlled discovery. In particular, poorly discriminative anomaly scores, limited effective calibration sample sizes, stricter or distribution-adaptive calibration regimes, and stringent error-control constraints can interact and compound, yielding few or no discoveries despite preserved validity guarantees. Through mathematical analysis, simulations, and empirical evaluations, we show that these failure modes are jointly governed by a multidimensional operational envelope. Our results demonstrate that conformal validity guarantees alone are insufficient to ensure operational utility: whether finite-sample guarantees translate into actionable discoveries depends jointly on the pipeline configuration, the available calibration information, and the underlying statistical regime. Our analysis characterizes the factors that govern the transition from formal validity to operational usefulness, clarifying failure modes and informing more effective system design.
